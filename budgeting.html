Budgeting Vocabulary

Budgeting

The foundation of all financial decisions; it is the relationship between income and expenses and identifies a plan moving forward.

Specifically, it is the process of creating a plan to spend, save, and manage money, essentially aligning income with expenses over a set period. It helps control spending, avoid debt, and prepare for financial goals.

How we talk about this:

A specific process, completed weekly, bi-weekly, or monthly where you:

  1. Track your income and expenses for the previous period (week, two weeks, or month).
  2. “Do the Math”—using a tool, identify if you had a surplus or a deficit last month.
  3. Make a plan using a budgeting model to see if your spending and income are in alignment with your financial goals.

Tools for tracking this can be simple or complex, but always involve these three steps.

Budgeting Vocabulary

Surplus

During the “Do the Math” step, you identify whether you had more than enough to cover your expenses. If you did, you have a “Surplus” that month—your income exceeds your expenses.

Info from Investopedia on Cashflow

Budgeting Vocabulary

Deficit

During the “Do the Math” step, you identify whether you had more than enough to cover your expenses. If your expenses exceeded your income, you had a deficit.

How we talk about this:

Deficits can be difficult to see as spending sometimes gets hidden within lines of debt, like credit cards. In that scenario, you might see your total debt owed go up each month, even though you’re still covering bills.

It’s the reason that doing the math and getting accurate data is important!

Info from Investopedia on Cashflow

Budgeting Vocabulary

Fixed Expense/Income

During the “Track Income/Expenses” step, you may want to identify which expenses are “Fixed”—meaning they can’t change. The way to reduce them is to get rid of the service or item.

This can be important to know during the “Planning” stage.

Info from Investopedia on Cashflow

Budgeting Vocabulary

Variable Expense/Income

During the “Track Income/Expenses” step, you may want to identify which expenses are “Variable”—meaning they can change month-to-month.

Variable income provides significant challenges and changes how people need to budget from a “one month at a time” to a full-year picture.

This can be important to know during the “Planning” stage!

Info from Investopedia on Cashflow

Real-World Connection

Someone may technically earn enough income to cover expenses but still struggle financially because bills are due before payday, unexpected costs come up, or spending habits are unclear.

Budgeting helps people understand patterns, prepare ahead, and make informed choices with the money they already have.

Bonus

Cashflow

Cash flow is the movement of money coming in and going out over time. Understanding cash flow helps people see when income is received, when expenses are due, and whether money is available when needed.

If we don’t account for cashflow—especially with variable income—we run the risk of having a budget that “looks correct” but not having enough cash when bills are due.

Using tools to understand when bills are due is important to make sure you don’t have a “Cash Flow” problem and accrue debt or fees accidentally.

Jordan’s Story

How does this impact Jordan?

Fixed income

Jordan works full time and usually has enough income to cover basic monthly expenses. His income is fixed—that is, he makes a predictable and largely the same amount of money each month.

Fixed vs. variable

Some of Jordan’s expenses are fixed, like rent, a phone bill, and a car payment because those amounts stay mostly the same each month. Other expenses are variable, like groceries, gas, and eating out, because those amounts can change.

What Jordan notices

At the end of one month, Jordan realizes there is very little money left before the next paycheck arrives. After writing out income and expenses, Jordan notices that several variable expenses were higher than expected that month, including takeout meals and increased gas costs.

Budgeting Models and Tools

Budgeting tools and models help organize spending and increase awareness, but they are not one-size-fits-all rules.

Budgeting Vocabulary

Budgeting Models

Budgeting models, like the 50/30/20 framework, are tools used to organize spending categories and support planning. They are meant to provide guidance, not strict rules.

Budgeting Vocabulary

50/30/20 Budget Model

The 50/30/20 model suggests:

  • 50% for needs
  • 30% for flexible spending
  • 20% for savings or debt repayment

This model is best used as a reference point rather than a requirement, especially for households with high living costs or limited income.

Video—50-30-20 Overview

Budgeting Vocabulary

Spending Plan

A spending plan is a budgeting approach that focuses on actual income, real expenses, and making adjustments over time based on a person’s real financial situation. Instead of trying to immediately fit into “ideal” budgeting percentages, a spending plan helps people understand what is currently happening with their money and identify realistic areas for change.

A spending plan is meant to be flexible and practical. It recognizes that income, expenses, and priorities may change from month to month, especially for households experiencing irregular income, high living costs, caregiving responsibilities, or unexpected expenses.

Unlike rigid budgeting systems, a spending plan encourages awareness, planning, and gradual improvement rather than restriction, and can be difficult to follow.

Video—Spending Plan—FFTP

Budgeting Vocabulary

Irregular Income

Irregular income may require different budgeting approaches, such as prioritizing essential expenses first, averaging income, or planning around lower-income months.

Video—Budgeting Irregular Income

Budgeting Vocabulary

Spending Patterns

Spending patterns matter more than individual purchases. Looking at repeated habits over time often provides more useful information than focusing on one-time expenses.

Jordan’s Story

How does Jordan budget?

Jordan recently moved to a new apartment after a rent increase at the previous place.

Although the move improved safety and reduced commuting time, the new rent and moving costs caused housing expenses to take up a much larger portion of monthly income than before.

The model Jordan started with

When he graduated high school and moved out, Jordan followed the 50/30/20 budgeting model:

  • 50% toward needs
  • 30% toward flexible spending
  • 20% toward savings and debt repayment
Why the model stopped fitting:

After reviewing actual expenses now that he lives in a different city, Jordan realized the numbers did not realistically fit the current situation. Rent, utilities, transportation, and minimum debt payments alone were already using more than half of monthly income. Trying to force spending into the model felt discouraging and unrealistic.

Jordan’s shift:

Instead of giving up on budgeting entirely, Jordan shifted to creating a spending plan based on actual income and real expenses.

Jordan began:
  • tracking monthly spending patterns,
  • identifying fixed versus variable expenses,
  • planning ahead for larger bills,
  • reducing a few flexible expenses temporarily,
  • and focusing on small, consistent financial improvements instead of “perfect” percentages.
Three-account strategy

Jordan used this information to start allocating his paycheck to 3 accounts—one for bills, one for savings, and one for “fun stuff.” When he has extra in one, he moves it to savings, and when he needs to cover bills, he moves money from his emergency savings.

Over time, the spending plan helped Jordan feel more aware and in control of financial decisions, even though the budget did not match a standard model exactly.

Financial Trade-Offs & Decision-Making

Budgeting involves making choices about how limited money is used. Financial decisions often involve competing priorities and trade-offs.

Budgeting Vocabulary

Trade-Offs

A trade-off happens when choosing one financial priority means there is less money available for something else. Because income is limited, people regularly make decisions about what needs to happen now, what can wait, and what may need to be reduced or adjusted.

Trade-offs are a normal part of budgeting and financial decision-making. They are not necessarily signs of failure or “bad” money management.

Key Idea: A trade-off is the choice between competing uses of money.

Video—Trade Offs (Business Perspective)

Budgeting Vocabulary

Opportunity Cost

Opportunity cost is the value or benefit that is given up when one choice is made instead of another. While trade-offs focus on the decision itself, opportunity cost focuses on what is lost, delayed, or missed because of that decision.

Opportunity cost helps explain why financial decisions can feel complicated, even when there is no clearly “wrong” answer.

Key Idea: Opportunity cost is the potential benefit or cost that could have come from choosing differently.

Video—Opportunity Costs Explained

Budgeting Vocabulary

Prioritization

Financial decisions are rarely about “right vs. wrong.” More often, they reflect what a person is prioritizing right now and what may need to be delayed.

Info from Investopedia on Cashflow

Budgeting Vocabulary

Limited Income & Decision Pressure

When income is limited, trade-offs become more difficult because financial decisions may directly affect stability, flexibility, or immediate needs.

Info from Investopedia on Cashflow

Jordan’s Story

Jordan’s got money problems…

Jordan’s work hours were reduced for several weeks after business slowed down unexpectedly. Even though the reduction was temporary, the lower income quickly created pressure on the monthly budget. After paying rent, utilities, transportation, minimum debt payments, and groceries, there is very little money remaining for anything else.

Four needs. One limited pool of money.

Jordan realizes that several important needs are competing for the same limited income:

  • catching up on a credit card balance,
  • replacing worn tires on the car,
  • rebuilding emergency savings,
  • and covering an upcoming medical bill.
Jordan has to prioritize:

Because there is not enough money to fully address everything at once, Jordan decides the tires are the most urgent expense because reliable transportation is necessary to continue getting to work safely.

This creates a trade-off:

Using available money for the tires means delaying additional credit card payments and postponing savings contributions.

There is also an opportunity cost:

By choosing the car repair now, Jordan gives up the opportunity to reduce debt faster or increase emergency savings in the short term.

As the weeks continue, Jordan notices how limited income increases decision pressure. Even relatively small financial choices begin to feel stressful because every dollar has multiple possible uses.

Purchases that once felt routine now require more planning and consideration.

The ongoing deficit

At the same time, Jordan’s monthly expenses are still slightly higher than income. This ongoing deficit means credit card balances continue increasing to cover gaps between paychecks.

Jordan recognizes that continuing to spend more than is coming in may lead to larger debt payments and even less flexibility in future months.

Jordan’s spending plan focuses on:
  • covering essential expenses first,
  • reducing unnecessary borrowing where possible,
  • tracking spending patterns,
  • and making gradual adjustments over time.

Although the situation is still stressful, understanding prioritization, trade-offs, opportunity cost, and deficit spending helps Jordan make decisions more intentionally rather than reacting only in moments of crisis.

Budgeting: TL;DR;

A budget is not a test, a punishment, or a perfect formula.

It is a tool that helps people understand how money is moving through their lives so they can make more informed decisions over time.

Most people are balancing:

  • fixed expenses,
  • changing priorities,
  • unexpected costs,
  • debt obligations,
  • and limited income or flexibility.

That means budgeting is often less about “getting the numbers perfect” and more about:

  • recognizing spending patterns,
  • planning ahead when possible,
  • understanding trade-offs,
  • and adjusting over time as life changes.

Budgeting models like 50/30/20 can be helpful starting points, but real life does not always fit neatly into percentages—especially in high-cost communities like Monterey County or during stressful financial periods.

Progress in budgeting often looks like:

  • fewer financial surprises,
  • better awareness,
  • more intentional decisions,
  • and increased confidence over time.

A useful budget is not the “perfect” budget.
It is the budget someone can realistically understand, use, and return to consistently.
