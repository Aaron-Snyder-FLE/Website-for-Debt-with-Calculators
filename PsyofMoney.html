Understanding Change Readiness

When we're delivering workshops - our audience is in varying states of “Change Readiness”

This often informs if someone is going to be engaged in a workshop- and whether or not they will come back to a future workshop.

We'll look at this through the lens of the “Stages of Change” - and see what this might look like in your audience!

There are lots of ways to think about “change readiness” and this is not an exhaustive list - but it's a great place to start!

Stage 1 1. Pre-Contemplation “I’m not thinking about this yet.”

In the precontemplation stage, individuals do not intend to take action in the foreseeable future, often defined as the next six months.

This is frequently due to limited awareness, past unsuccessful attempts, or a belief that change is not relevant or possible. Research shows that individuals in this stage may actively avoid information or reinterpret it to reduce discomfort (a form of cognitive dissonance reduction).

In financial contexts, this can be tied to learned helplessness or systemic barriers that make change feel unrealistic.

What this looks like in a workshop:

  • Completes activities quickly or superficially, often without applying them to their own situation
  • Gives short, general, or deflective responses when invited to share
  • May use humor or dismissal to distance themselves from the topic (“This doesn’t really apply to me”)
  • Engagement increases when content feels highly relatable or low-pressure

Example:

Chris signed up for the workshop because their employer required it as part of a benefits program. They don’t think their finances are something they can realistically change right now and mostly plan to sit through it and get the credit. If anything, they’re hoping it won’t be a waste of time.

Stage 2 2. Contemplation “I’m thinking about it, but I’m not ready yet.”

Individuals in the contemplation stage are aware of a problem and are seriously considering change but remain ambivalent.

This stage is characterized by weighing perceived benefits against perceived costs, often resulting in decisional paralysis. Research indicates that people can remain in this stage for extended periods, particularly when the perceived barriers (time, effort, emotional discomfort) outweigh the benefits.

In financial literacy, this often appears as awareness of debt or spending issues without clear movement toward action. Effective strategies emphasize resolving ambivalence and increasing self-efficacy rather than pushing immediate behavior change.

What this looks like in a workshop:

  • Actively listening (nodding, reacting to examples, following along closely)
  • Asks general or exploratory questions rather than action-oriented ones
  • Participates in activities but hesitates when asked to make firm decisions or commitments
  • Reflects on their situation but often speaks in “I probably should…” or “maybe someday…” language
  • Shows curiosity paired with uncertainty or ambivalence

Example:

Maria has had a few months where she's had a hard time making ends meet - she missed a few bills because of a cash-flow problem. When Salinas Valley Promise offered financial workshop series, she signed up for the credit, and picked a topic related to budgeting.

She comes into the No suggestions workshop thinking “I know I should track my spending… I just don’t know where to start. Maybe this will help me think about what to do".

Stage 3 3. Preparation “I’m getting ready to take action.”

The preparation stage represents a transition from intention to behavior, where individuals plan to take action in the near term (typically within 30 days) and may begin small steps.

At this point, self-efficacy tends to increase, but individuals still benefit from structured guidance and support. Research shows that clear goal-setting and implementation planning significantly improve follow-through during this stage.

In financial behavior change, this may include opening a budgeting app, gathering account information, or setting initial financial goals. Interventions are most effective when they reduce complexity and provide actionable, step-by-step guidance - which our workshops can introduce, but are best supported by coaching.

What this looks like in a workshop:

  • Engaged and focused, often leaning into activities with intention
  • Asks practical, “how do I actually do this?” questions
  • Begins applying concepts directly to their own situation during exercises
  • May seek validation or reassurance that they are “doing it right”
  • Leaves activities with rough plans, notes, or partially completed tools

Example:

Luis signed up intentionally because he wants to get his finances on track before moving out on his own. He’s been thinking about budgeting for a while and has even downloaded an app but hasn’t followed through with implementing a budget plan yet. He’s hoping this workshop will give him a clear place to start - and he'd really benefit from coaching.

Stage 4 4. Action “I’m actively making changes.”

In the action stage, individuals have recently begun modifying their behavior and are actively implementing change strategies. This stage requires the greatest investment of time and energy.

Behavioral research highlights the importance of reinforcement, feedback, and problem-solving during this phase to sustain momentum.

In financial contexts, this could include actively tracking expenses, paying down debt, or building savings habits. Support during this stage should focus on troubleshooting barriers and reinforcing progress rather than introducing entirely new systems. This is often done through financial coaching, not our workshops.

What this looks like in a workshop:

  • Frequently connects content to things they are already doing (“I tried that last month…”)
  • Shares real examples of successes and challenges
  • Uses activities to refine or troubleshoot existing systems rather than start from scratch
  • Asks specific, targeted questions about improving outcomes
  • Open about setbacks and interested in solutions

Example:

Maya enrolled in the workshop after already starting to track her spending over the past month - and has implemented a budget plan. She’s here to refine what she’s already doing and make it more sustainable - but realizes after the workshop that she's on the right track. She was also able to share what she could do to help other which made her even more confident in her ability.

Stage 5 5. Maintenance “I’ve made changes and I’m sustaining them.”

What this looks like in a workshop:

  • Completes activities quickly due to already having systems in place
  • Contributes examples, insights, or encouragement that benefit others
  • Engages more in discussion than in basic skill-building tasks
  • Focuses on optimization, long-term planning, or edge cases
  • May naturally take on a peer-support role within the group

Example:

Ethan joined the workshop voluntarily for credit even though he already feels confident managing his money. He’s been budgeting and saving consistently but wants to make sure he’s not missing anything and to stay on track long-term. He’s also open to picking up small improvements or new strategies.

Most participants do not arrive to our workshops ready to take immediate action.

Our feedback shows that many individuals in our workshops are in early stages of readiness (Precontemplation or Contemplation).

This means they may not yet see change as possible, or they may be thinking about it but feel unsure, overwhelmed, or not ready to act.

In our programs, this is not viewed as a barrier; it is our starting point.

What This Means in Practice

Many participants:

  • Did not choose to attend this workshop or are doing so for credit
  • Are managing real financial stress and constraints alongside other issues that compete for their time
  • May have had negative or judgmental experiences with money or financial systems, particularly for participants who immigrated to the US
  • Are stressed and don't really know where to start tackling issues in their finance
  • May have very little background in US specific financial systems (Credit, Savings/Retirement Accounts, etc.)

Because of this, our workshops are designed to prioritize:

  • Psychological safety over performance
  • Relevance over rigid instruction
  • Understanding over immediate action

Behavior change does not happen simply because someone receives information. Pushing participants into action too quickly can increase stress, reinforce feelings of failure, and create resistance to the content.

Instead, we focus on helping participants make sense of what they are experiencing by:

  • Explaining what financial concepts mean
  • Normalizing common experiences
  • Creating space for reflection without pressure
  • Providing tools for follow up - giving the reigns back to the participant - allowing them to access the tools when THEY want to.

This approach shows up in how our workshops are delivered.

You may notice that we:

  • Use examples and discussion more than step-by-step instruction
  • Don't ask anyone to fill out a worksheet on their own
  • Do not require personal sharing
  • Avoid presenting a single “right” way to manage money

These choices are intentional. They help participants stay engaged without feeling judged or overwhelmed.

What Success Looks Like

Success is not immediate behavior change.

Success looks like participants leaving the workshop thinking:

  • “That felt relevant to me”
  • “I’m not the only one dealing with this”
  • “This is something I could think more about”
  • “I could probably achieve that goal I wrote about using the tools that I have”
  • “I want to learn more about what change might look like”

These shifts are the foundation for meaningful engagement and change over time.

What about someone who is ready to change or has made a change?

🧭

“I Want to Get Started” (Preparation Stage)

Participants may:

  • Ask “how do I start?” or “what should I do first?”
  • Share that they’ve been meaning to take action but haven’t yet

How to respond:

  • Acknowledge their readiness without prescribing specific steps
  • Use language like: “That’s a great place to explore further—this is exactly the kind of thing financial coaching can support.”
  • Call out the tools provided by the workshop folder as a “Toolkit” with lots of resources.
  • Encourage them to connect with FEC Financial Coaching for structured, one-on-one support
🔧

“I’m Already Trying This” (Action Stage)

Participants may:

  • Share what they’ve already started (budgeting, saving, paying down debt)
  • Ask for help troubleshooting or improving their approach

How to respond:

  • Validate their effort and normalize challenges
  • Encourage them!
  • Share that there are lot of resources - finance for the people, tools from the CFPB, that can help them align their goals with their finances.
  • Avoid giving personalized advice in the group setting
  • Redirect to coaching: “You’re already doing a lot. Financial coaching can help you refine that with someone who can look at your full situation.”
🔄

“I Want to Stay on Track” (Maintenance Stage)

Participants may:

  • Express concern about consistency or setbacks
  • Ask about long-term strategies or “what’s next”

How to respond:

  • Reinforce what they’re already doing well
  • Highlight that ongoing support is part of sustaining progress
  • Position coaching as a way to maintain momentum and adapt over time
